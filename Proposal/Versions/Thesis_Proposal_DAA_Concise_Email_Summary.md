# Typographic Archives of Latino Life: New Media Alphabets in Southwestern Ontario

**Daniel Achig-Aranha**  
**OCAD University, Interdisciplinary Master’s in Art, Media and Design**  
**Concise thesis proposal summary - May 2026**


## Project overview

*Typographic Archives of Latino Life: New Media Alphabets in Southwestern Ontario* is a research-creation thesis about typography, Latino life, memory, place, and ethical representation. The project focuses on Latino residents along the London-Sarnia corridor in Southwestern Ontario, where Latin American presence is measurable and growing. According to 2021 Census data included in the full proposal, London’s Latin American population increased from 9,045 in 2016 to 12,580 in 2021, while Sarnia’s Latin American population increased from 390 in 2016 to 620 in 2021.

The thesis proposes the development of a co-designed expanded Latin character set shaped by conversations, visual mapping, participant-informed references, typographic testing, and material production. The project treats typography not only as a formal design outcome, but also as a method of listening, collecting, translating, testing, and making visible situated forms of experience.

The aim is not to create a decorative “Latino style.” Instead, the thesis asks how letterforms can emerge from lived experiences, visual references, migration memories, storefront graphics, domestic objects, handwritten marks, routes, language practices, and everyday visual culture without reducing those experiences to fixed cultural symbols.

## Central research question

How can a co-designed expanded Latin character set developed with Latino residents along the London-Sarnia corridor document everyday life, challenge dominant assumptions about typographic quality, and support ethical forms of visibility and representation?

## Key areas of inquiry

The project is organized through six connected areas:

1. **Typography as archive:** How can letterforms function as testimony, memory, evidence, and cultural record?
2. **Who defines “good typography”?:** How are ideas of clarity, professionalism, legibility, and correctness shaped by power, institutions, and design education?
3. **Typographic mechanics:** How do stroke, rhythm, spacing, hierarchy, accents, diacritics, punctuation, scale, and paragraph texture shape the character set as a working typographic system?
4. **Ethical visibility:** How can the project support representation without becoming extractive, reductive, or disconnected from participant agency?
5. **Corridor life as visual ecology:** How do routes, labour, storefronts, homes, churches, schools, roads, service spaces, and digital communication shape visual memory along the London-Sarnia corridor?
6. **Traditional printing as material research:** How can letterpress and related printing processes test the character set through pressure, surface, texture, repetition, editioning, and public display?

## Methodology

The methodology is research-creation, supported by primary research, secondary research, participatory design, visual mapping, type design, traditional printing, documentation, and reflexive writing.

**Primary research** will include semi-structured conversations, visual mapping, participatory typographic workshops, participant review, type testing, and material experiments. These methods will help identify the words, places, memories, signs, objects, routes, and visual references that shape the expanded Latin character set.

**Participatory typographic workshops** will allow participants to respond to letterforms, compare visual directions, discuss typographic clarity, identify what feels familiar or forced, and review what should or should not become public. This shifts participants from being only sources of material to being evaluators and co-authors of meaning.

**Type design and typographic testing** will include sketching, digitizing, drawing outlines, spacing, kerning, testing accents and diacritics, generating proofs, and evaluating the character set across print, screen, motion, specimens, and posters.

**Traditional printing and letterpress poster production** will be used as supervised material research. The goal is to test how the expanded Latin character set changes when it moves from digital outline into paper, ink, plate, pressure, repetition, and edition. This process also connects the project to labour, craft knowledge, touch, memory, and my family history with print shops across more than three generations.

**Reflexive writing** will be used throughout the project to examine my role as designer and researcher. It will ask what I choose, what I translate, what may be lost, who approves the form, and what should remain private, unprinted, or unnamed.

## Secondary research and references

The secondary research combines typography, critical theory, Latinx design history, diaspora studies, ethics, place-based research, and print culture.

Key theoretical and methodological references include:

- **Typography and discourse:** Rob Waller, Sofie Beier, Herbert Spencer.
- **Typographic mechanics:** Gerrit Noordzij, Jost Hochuli, Cyrus Highsmith.
- **Language, orientation, and embodied perception:** Gloria Anzaldúa, Sara Ahmed, Maurice Merleau-Ponty, James J. Gibson.
- **Ethics and decolonial design:** Donna Haraway, Arturo Escobar, Schultz et al., Rose-Redwood et al.
- **Diaspora, hybridity, place, and surface:** Kalra, Hutnyk, Kaur, Zakiya Luna, Giuliana Bruno.
- **Print, reproduction, and material texts:** Walter Benjamin, D. F. McKenzie, Johanna Drucker, Dori Griffin, Archer-Parré and Mussell.

A unique secondary research source is BIPOC Design History’s online course *Incomplete Latinx Stories of Diseño Gráfico: Borderlands/La Frontera*. This audiovisual material strengthens the thesis because it brings Latinx and Latin American design histories into the bibliography through diasporic, community-centred, and critical perspectives.

Relevant classes from the course include:

- **Arte y Diseño Latinx: Comunicación Cotidiana** for everyday visual culture, local materials, language, and audience.
- **Some Theoretical Considerations in Reading Latin American Design History** for regional design theory and critique.
- **Latinx Diaspora in America** for migration, hybrid culture, identity, print, and protest.
- **Diseñando Identidad** for design, community education, politics, and Puerto Rican visual culture.
- **Caribbean Contrast** for Puerto Rican and Cuban posters as public, political, and cultural communication.
- **Latinx Identities and Self-Reflection in Traditional Design Learning Spaces** for positionality, design education, and reflexive methods.
- **On Typography and Language in Peru** for typography, language, resistance, revolution, and liberation.
- **Otros Susurros Desde los Andes** for translating cultural visual signs into contemporary typographic form through semiotic and morphological analysis.

## Expected outputs

The thesis will include two major components: a research-creation exhibition and a written thesis paper.

The exhibition may include:

- A co-designed expanded Latin character set or type family.
- Type specimens and typographic tests.
- A traditional printing or letterpress poster series.
- Participant-informed word, place, and route studies.
- Process documentation, including maps, sketches, proofs, revisions, plates, and print tests.
- A digital or motion-based specimen showing the character set across screen-based or variable conditions.

The written thesis will explain the theoretical framework, methodology, ethical structure, making process, analysis, and contribution of the project.

## Significance

This project contributes to typography by treating type design as a situated and participatory research practice. It shows that an expanded Latin character set can carry memory, place, visual culture, labour, language, and social relationships.

It contributes to Latino representation in Southwestern Ontario by focusing on a regional context that is often less visible than larger metropolitan centres. The London-Sarnia corridor is understood not as a neutral location, but as a lived visual ecology shaped by migration, movement, labour, family, community, service spaces, and everyday design.

It contributes to ethical and decolonial design practice by questioning universal standards of “good typography,” centering participant review, and treating visibility as something that must be negotiated carefully through consent, authorship, anonymity, and circulation.

It contributes to printmaking and material culture by using traditional printing not as nostalgia, but as a research method. Letterpress and related processes allow the character set to be tested through paper, ink, pressure, texture, editioning, and public presentation.

## Working contribution

The thesis proposes a model for co-designed typography that connects Latino life, expanded Latin character set design, participatory research, audiovisual Latinx design history, traditional printing, and ethical representation. The project asks how typography can become not only a visual system, but also a method of archiving lived experience and making place, memory, and cultural presence visible through letters.
